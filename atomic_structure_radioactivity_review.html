01 · Know the particles

Inside an atom

ParticleChargeLocationApproximate mass
Proton+1Nucleus1 amu
Neutron0Nucleus1 amu
Electron−1Outside nucleusNearly 0 amu
Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. This determines which element it is.
Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. This is a whole number for one particular atom.

Important: The decimal atomic mass printed on a periodic table is a weighted average of an element’s naturally occurring isotopes. It is not the mass number of every atom of that element.

02 · Read the symbol

Nuclide symbols

A nuclide symbol gives an element’s mass number, atomic number, and sometimes electrical charge.

AZXcharge

A = mass number (upper left)
Z = atomic number (lower left)
X = element symbol
Charge = upper right, if the atom is an ion

Worked example

2311Na+
  • Protons = 11
  • Neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12
  • Electrons = 11 − 1 = 10
  • Mass number = 23
  • Charge = +1
Neutrons = A − ZCharge = protons − electronsElectrons = protons − charge

When using the last formula, treat the charge as a signed number: for a 2− ion, electrons = protons − (−2) = protons + 2.

03 · Spot the difference

Isotopes and ions

Isotopes

Atoms of the same element with the same protons but different neutrons. They have different mass numbers.

Example: carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have 6 protons; they have 6 and 8 neutrons, respectively.

Ions

Atoms with an electrical charge because electrons have been gained or lost. Protons do not change when an ordinary ion forms.

Positive ion: loses electrons. Negative ion: gains electrons.

Memory check: Change neutrons → new isotope. Change electrons → ion. Change protons → different element.
04 · Nuclear changes

Radiation and radioactivity

An unstable nucleus may spontaneously change (radioactive decay) and release radiation. Nuclear decay changes the nucleus; ordinary chemical reactions rearrange electrons and bonds.

EmissionWhat leaves the nucleus?Nuclear symbolEffect on parent nucleusTypical shielding
Alpha (α)2 protons + 2 neutrons⁴₂He or ⁴₂αA decreases by 4; Z decreases by 2Paper or skin
Beta-minus (β⁻)High-speed electron created when a neutron becomes a proton⁰₋₁e or ⁰₋₁βA stays the same; Z increases by 1Thin aluminum or plastic
Gamma (γ)High-energy electromagnetic radiation (photon)⁰₀γNo change to A or ZThick lead or concrete reduces exposure

Shielding comparisons are simplified. Actual protection depends on radiation energy, exposure time, and whether the material is outside or inside the body. All three types can be hazardous.

Do not confuse beta emission with losing an orbital electron. In β⁻ decay, a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton, and an electron is emitted from the nucleus.
05 · Show the nuclear reaction

Writing balanced nuclear equations

Both sides must have equal total mass numbers (top numbers) and total atomic numbers (bottom numbers). Atomic number tells you which element the daughter nucleus becomes.

Alpha decay example

23892U → 23490Th + 42He

Check: 238 = 234 + 4, and 92 = 90 + 2. The daughter element has atomic number 90, thorium.

Beta-minus decay example

146C → 147N + 0−1e

Check: 14 = 14 + 0, and 6 = 7 + (−1). In β⁻ decay, the daughter nucleus has one more proton than the parent.

These are simplified classroom equations. Physically, beta-minus decay also emits an electron antineutrino, usually omitted in introductory chemistry nuclear equations.

How to solve: (1) Write the starting nuclide. (2) Add the emitted particle to the products. (3) Subtract mass numbers and atomic numbers to find the daughter. (4) Use the periodic table to identify its element.
06 · Interactive simulation

How half-life works

Half-life is the time it takes for half of the radioactive nuclei in a large sample to decay. After each half-life, half of those still radioactive remain, not half of the starting sample.

Idealized model: Remaining atoms = starting atoms × (½)number of half-lives. This model displays expected fractions; real radioactive atoms decay randomly, so a small sample will not always halve exactly.

Key question: If 400 atoms remain at the start, how many remain after three half-lives? 400 → 200 → 100 → 50. That is 12.5% of the original sample.

At higher half-life counts, the expected number of atoms may be fractional. Since actual atoms are whole particles and decay at random, the model rounds the visible dot count and displays the expectation to one decimal when needed.

07 · Practice

Check yourself

Try these without notes. Open each answer to check your work.

1. A neutral atom has 17 protons and 18 neutrons. Find its atomic number, mass number, electrons, and element.
Show answer

Z = 17, A = 17 + 18 = 35, electrons = 17, element = chlorine (Cl).

2. A magnesium ion has 12 protons, 12 neutrons, and 10 electrons. What is its charge and full nuclide symbol?
Show answer

Charge = 12 − 10 = 2+. Mass number = 24. Symbol: ²⁴₁₂Mg²⁺.

3. Are ¹²₆C and ¹⁴₆C isotopes, ions, or different elements? Explain.
Show answer

Isotopes: both have 6 protons, but carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8.

4. Finish the alpha-decay equation: ²²⁶₈₈Ra → _____ + ⁴₂He.
Show answer

²²²₈₆Rn (radon). Subtract 4 from the top number and 2 from the bottom number.

5. Finish the beta-minus equation: ³H (hydrogen-3) → _____ + ⁰₋₁e.
Show answer

³₂He (helium-3). Mass stays 3; atomic number increases from 1 to 2.

6. A 200 g sample has a half-life of 5 years. How much remains radioactive after 15 years?
Show answer

15 ÷ 5 = 3 half-lives. 200 → 100 → 50 → 25 g.

7. Does gamma emission change an atom’s mass number or atomic number?
Show answer

Neither. Gamma emission releases energy but does not remove protons or neutrons.

8. Why is chlorine’s atomic mass on the periodic table about 35.45 rather than a whole number?
Show answer

It is a weighted average of the masses of naturally occurring chlorine isotopes. A specific chlorine atom has a whole-number mass number, such as 35 or 37.
